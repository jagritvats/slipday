    What am I looking at? Where do the real landings' needs come from?

    Near the Moon's south pole the Sun and the Earth both stay within a few degrees of the horizon, so a ridge can hide them for days. Sunlight means solar power; the Earth in view means a direct radio link. Slipday ray-casts the real skyline from NASA's LOLA laser-altimeter elevation data and asks, for each day the landing could slip to, whether the mission still gets the sunlight and Earth contact it needs. It asks the same question at 13 points within 100 m of the target (or within one cell of the elevation grid, where that is larger), on a coarser elevation grid and with a finer sampling of the nearest ground: the answer is only "Yes" when all agree. The rows below it ask what happens if the landing is less precise than that.

    How sure the slope figures are

    NASA's own 5 m slope maps give about 2° of uncertainty per point, so a slope within about 2° of the 5° goal or the 8° limit is close to it. The opening example's steepest point is about 5°, right at NASA's 5° goal: moving it by one 5 m cell gives 4.4° to 5.8°. Of the 78 published Artemis region sites, 76 have only 80 m terrain here, which is too coarse to judge slope well. On the map's slope layer, "slip-tolerant" means at least 1 day of slip at all 13 touchdown points; the map's own legend also counts points that keep 0 days.

    What the safe-touchdown chances leave out

    The chances above use slope only: there is no boulder data. The three lander classes are assumed accuracies, each taken from one published figure, not the specification of any real lander. A 2° error in NASA's slope map changes the 5° figures a lot: for the opening example's precision lander, 90 % can fall to 23 %.
