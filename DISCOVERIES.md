# Discoveries

## Regions to keep open, 15 Nov 2026 (measured on that machine; DEEP_PASS_3.md)

1. **NASA's "more than one region" holds on open data.** On the 78 published candidate landing sites in NASA's
   nine regions (Wueller et al. 2026), at landing times every 6 hours from 2027 to 2030, a 6.25-day stay with
   the Sun up throughout and 100 hours of Earth contact: the best single region (Nobile Rim 1) is open at 28 %
   of the landing times, the best three at 40 %, all nine at 44 %. The illustrative 100-hour Earth need sets
   that ceiling: with sunlight only, 48 %, 91 %, 92 %. Shares of landing times, not of real launch
   opportunities. Fragile Yes results (11.9 % of all Yes results) are not counted.
2. **Our ephemeris gives the paper's midsummer dates to the day** for all eight years 2025 to 2032; the season
   moves 18.4 days earlier a year on average (18.63 expected from the eclipse year).
3. **Against the paper's own sunlight figures, a split.** Of 312 comparisons, about two in three agree within a
   day and about three in ten differ by more than 3 days, clustered by region and year (all nine Nobile Rim 2
   sites in 2027, for example), nearly always with our run the longer. Our terrain model (80 m) is coarser
   than the paper's (60 m). Not resolved.
4. **On published sites the Artemis option list has fewer fragile answers.** For the opening plan 8, 6 and 5 of the nine
   regions have a clear-yes landing within three days of the December, January and February windows (6, 5 and
   4 not fragile), against 7, 7 and 5 (3, 1 and 2 not fragile) with one point of ours per region.

## Deep pass, 15 Nov 2026 (measured on that machine; DEEP_PASS.md)

1. **Real slips step a month; the next window can lose the site, and the cause is the season.** At the
   opening example the planned window and the next are Yes (8 and 6 days of margin), the two after it No on
   sunlight: at the same time of the lunar day sunlight falls 314, 259, 217, 151 hours against 240 needed
   (corrected in the follow-up: the deep pass blamed Earth contact, which stays above its 100 hours). In
   January no landing hour within three days works there, but 13 of 20 other sites have a Yes hour within three
   days, 11 of them fragile. The steadiest is a map point at 89.498 S, 137.663 W (15 Jan 13:00, 4 days, Sun about
   0.3 degrees or more above the skyline at every touchdown point). C1's 14 Jan 01:00 landing reads 5 days, but two
   touchdown points sit within 0.08 degrees of the skyline (at touchdown, and on the second day of slip); a separate
   calculation puts the second just below it and gets 1 day. Its 16 Jan 19:00 landing gives 2 days. All fragile.
2. **IM-1's slip from January to February cost it little at Malapert A**: at the same time of the lunar day a
   modelled January landing is Yes with one day of margin, as in February; across the published January
   window, Yes from 21 Jan and No on 17 to 20 Jan (before local sunrise).
3. **A late-2027 VIPER landing at sunrise on Nobile's west rim (illustrative) fails on the Earth**, in every
   window from September to December: the Earth is 3.2 to 7.4 degrees below the skyline at touchdown.
4. **The night a late landing meets is the whole lunar night**: 179 hours for the opening example's last
   passing day, not the 21 hours inside its window that pass 4 reported.

## Fourth local pass, 15 Nov 2026 (measured on that machine)

1. **At the opening example the last passing slip day runs into the lunar night (corrected in the deep
   pass).** Landing on days 0 to 7 the lander meets at most a 20-hour night; landing on day 8, the last that
   passes, it meets a 179-hour night that begins inside its window. The Earth outage it meets is one 323-hour
   Earth-down period on every passing day. Pass 4 reported the clipped parts (21 h and 27 to 219 h) as the
   headline; the review showed that understated what a late landing must outlast.
2. **Across the map, the worst whole night over the passing slip days is 120 hours or more at 568 of 574
   points** (range 14 to 468 h); the opening point's is 179 h.
3. The run-length code reproduces the existing longest-darkness figures at the Shackleton ridge: 194 h on
   80 m, 110 h on 20 m (published 112 h).
4. **The 80 m map's optimism holds across the season.** Re-checked on 20 m for 14 weekly map dates, the share
   that agrees is 72 to 89 % on the 7 dates with any passing point (86.6 % in all); 452 of 454 disagreements
   are points the 80 m map keeps and the 20 m grid loses. On the other 7 dates no map point passes at all.
5. **Naming corrected.** The ground that ends the opening margin is at 98.6 % of Malapert crater's radius (its
   rim) and 78 % of Mons Mouton's; under the pass-4 rule it is "inside Mons Mouton".

## Third local pass, 15 Nov 2026 (measured on that machine)

Commands and outputs: LOCAL_PASS_2_RESULTS.md, "Pass 3". Rules R1 to R4 were written there before the
answers they govern were computed.

1. **The opening example's Yes rests on 0.64 degrees of Earth clearance.** At touchdown the Earth's centre
   stands 0.64 degrees above the skyline (the thinnest of the example's nine yes-days). The Yes holds up
   to a link mask of 0.60 degrees, is Not sure from 0.65 and No from 0.70. No published elevation mask for
   a direct-to-Earth link from the lunar surface was found, so masks are labelled illustrative. At an
   illustrative 1 degree, the first candidate in the fixed order that stays a clear Yes on both grids is
   the sixth (88.886 S, 121.691 E: Yes, 6 days on 80 m, 5 on 20 m); the opening example was not changed.
2. **NASA's ground stations do not cut the example's contact.** With an hour counted only when Goldstone,
   Madrid or Canberra has the Moon at least 10 degrees up (DSN 810-005, cited; geometry within 0.0006
   degrees of JPL Horizons), Goldstone carries 104 hours of the first 14 days, Madrid 122 and Canberra 85,
   and none is lost: verdict and margin unchanged on both grids. Over the 18.6-year cycle the hours with
   no complex in view stay at 0.3 to 1.5 %; Canberra's share moves from 26 to 39 %.
3. **Twenty map points survive a slip beside permanent shadow; none keeps five days.** 10 ha or more of
   permanent shadow within 1 km, a margin of a day or more at all 13 touchdown points, the point not on
   shadow itself: 20 points (7 more stand on shadow). The best, 89.572 S, 19.134 E, keeps 4 days on both
   the 80 m and the 20 m grid. Permanent shadow is where it is dark, not where ice is.
4. **On the 20 m grid the slip-margin map is optimistic where it is wrong.** For the default date 639 of
   733 re-checked points (87.2 %) give the same answer; all 94 that differ keep a margin on 80 m and lose
   it on 20 m. Margins are equal at 573 points. 4 of the 20 shadow sites lose the planned date on 20 m.
5. **The ground that ends the example's margin** lies 131.6 km west-south-west, at 78 % of Mons Mouton's
   radius in the IAU gazetteer (so "inside Mons Mouton" under the naming rule of pass 4) and at 98.6 % of
   Malapert crater's radius, on its rim line. Pass 3 called it "inside Malapert"; that overstated it.

## Second local pass, 14 Nov 2026, late (measured on that machine)

Commands and outputs for every figure: LOCAL_PASS_2_RESULTS.md. Two of these correct what stands below.

1. **The headline example does not survive a finer grid unchanged (a correction).** On NASA's 20 m LOLA
   mosaic near the pole the default example (connecting ridge C1, landing 18 Nov 2026) reads "Not sure" on
   the planned date: 11 of 13 touchdown points pass, two lose the Earth at touchdown. Slips of 1 to 7
   days pass at all 13 on both grids. On the 80 m grid the page uses, the planned date is "Yes" (13 of
   13). Clear-yes landing dates in a year: 35 on 20 m, 62 on 80 m. So finding 2 below ("100 % within
   100 m") is an 80 m figure: on 20 m it is 88 %.
2. **At that example the margin is ended by the Earth, not the Sun.** Day 8 fails because one touchdown
   point 100 m north-west of the target gets 97 hours of Earth contact of the 100 needed; the Sun is up
   for the whole window at every point. The 100 hours are an illustrative need, so the 7-day margin is
   set by an illustrative number. The ground that hides the Earth is the crest the lander stands on, one
   grid cell away.
3. **A fifth of the slip map does not survive the landing scatter.** Of 733 map points that pass on the
   planned date, 574 keep a margin at all 13 touchdown points and 159 lose it. A margin of five days or
   more survives at 18 points, not 74. This replaces the 12-point sample of finding 3 below with the
   whole map (13 points only: no coarser grid, no near-field variant).
4. **The finer grid agrees with the published darkness figure.** Longest darkness at the published ridge
   point: 110 h on the 20 m grid against a published 112 h; the 80 m grid gives 194 h. Time with any Sun
   there: 92.4 % on 20 m, 92.5 % on 80 m, 92.1 % published. The spread within 100 m is wider on the
   finer grid (64 to 92 % against 74 to 93 %): the knife edge is sharper than the 80 m grid shows.
5. **"20 yes against 20 yes" hid four disagreements.** At C1 a smooth sphere and the terrain both give 20
   clear-yes dates in 60, but not the same ones: they differ on four dates (two each way), and terrain is
   "not sure" on six more. On high ground terrain can add light as well as take it away.
6. **For a wider landing dispersion a later date can be the better plan.** With the assumed CLPS-class
   dispersion (one standard deviation 250 m) the chance that the plan holds at C1 is 35 % on the planned
   date and 72 % six or seven days later, then nothing on day 9.
7. **The map's best scatter-robust point is also robust to the grid.** Chosen by a written rule,
   the first candidate tried (89.010 S, 122.764 E, the top of the slip-robust list) reads Yes with 8 days
   of margin on the 80 m grid and on the 20 m grid, at 13 of 13 touchdown points on both, for the landing
   of 18 Nov 2026. Its margin ends on day 9 for the same reason on both grids: the Earth sets behind high
   ground 131.6 km away and leaves 95 hours of contact. One site and one date; the other 17 candidates
   were not tried.

## Local pass, 14 Nov 2026 (official NASA downloads; measured on that machine)

Commands and outputs for every figure: LOCAL_PASS_RESULTS.md.

1. **The Earth side validates as well as the Sun side.** Against the LOLA team's Earth-visibility maps at
   the same 400 seeded points: 0.97 points mean absolute difference (correlation 0.9946) on the 60 m map
   and 0.69 (0.9984) on the 120 m map, with the rule the maps' own labels state (any part of the Earth's
   disc). The Sun figures at those points are 0.80 and 0.76. The definition matters as much as the
   terrain: judged by the Earth's centre, the same model is 3.9 and 4.1 points off; by the whole disc, 7.7
   and 7.9. On the 60 well-lit ridge pixels where the Sun check degrades to 4.0 points (correlation 0.61),
   the Earth check holds at 1.57 points (correlation 0.97). That suggests Earth visibility on a crest
   depends less on fine terrain than sunlight does; the reason was not investigated.
2. **A landing circle turns the knife edge into a number.** At the connecting ridge C1 (18 Nov 2026,
   default need) the plan holds at 100 % of touchdown points within 100 m, 62 % within 250 m, 27 % within
   500 m and 13 % within 1 km. At the IM-2 site the same four figures are 100, 100, 100 and 96 %; at
   IM-1's (its seven-day plan) 100, 100, 98 and 88 %. The best-lit ridge is the least forgiving place to
   miss; the plateau sites forgive almost anything.
3. **The slip map's best point is not its most robust one.** Of the 12 map points with the largest
   single-point margin (8 or 9 days), 5 stay a clear yes when re-checked with the 13-point scatter. The
   map's top point (9 days, 89.774 S 56.889 E) drops to "not sure" (11 of 13). The most robust is 89.010 S
   122.764 E: yes at all 13 points, 8 days of margin, 16 clear-yes dates and none unsure. (What that
   feature is called was not looked up.)
4. **IM-1's own plan fits; a longer one would not have.** With about seven days of surface operations
   (168 sunlit hours) the card says yes with one day of slip margin. Terrain leaves 208 sunlit hours in
   the 14 days after the real landing hour, so NASA's press-kit figure of approximately 10 days (240 h)
   gives No. A flat horizon finds 17 good landing dates in 60 for the seven-day plan; terrain leaves 9.
5. **A full-lunar-day plan has no slip margin, by arithmetic.** Blue Ghost's 14-day plan needs every hour
   of the window lit: yes on the landing date, no slip margin, next windows one and two months later.
6. **A 240 m grid cannot vouch for a 100 m scatter.** With the scatter widened to one cell the fallback
   stops reading "yes" at the two ridge sites the review named, and it also stops reading "yes" at C1.
   That is the same answer the 80 m grid gives at a 200 to 250 m scatter (6 or 7 of 13 points pass).
7. **The arena limit is the whole difference at 512 MB, and with it the 80 m grid fits too.** In a 512 MB
   container the 240 m server is killed under the load test without the limit and peaks at 296 to 299 MB
   of group memory with it. The 80 m server, with the limit, answered the same load test inside 512 MB
   with a group peak of 431 MB: its larger resident size is the memory-mapped grid, which the kernel
   drops under a limit. So the small host does not force the fallback; the 231 MB file only has to be
   fetched at build time. Container figures from one machine, not from a hosting provider.
8. **Nothing in the mirrored data was wrong.** All seven official downloads with a recorded hash matched;
   two labels differ only in line endings; the crop of the SVS grid and the rebuilt 14,400-skyline field
   are byte-identical to the earlier ones; the 218 relayed Horizons rows equal the direct download.

# Discoveries, round 3

What was learned while building, for the choice between workstreams. Numbers are measured in this build
unless a source is named.

## Findings in the real data

1. **The published best point is a knife edge.** At the Shackleton connecting ridge (C1, 2 m) the model
   reproduces the published 92.1 % of time with Sun at the stated coordinates. Twelve points 50 and 100 m
   away give 72.5 to 92.5 %; for the 365 days from 18 Nov 2026 the range is 74.0 to 93.0 %, and the longest
   darkness runs from 6 to 21 days. A landing-site number quoted for a single ridge pixel means little
   without the scatter around it. This is the entry's strongest honest point: it is true whatever grid a
   competitor uses, and nobody else read shows it.
2. **The slip map found the LOLA team's best pixel by itself.** Asked for the point with the most good
   landing dates in 60 (need: 240 sunlit hours and 100 hours of Earth contact in 14 days, from 18 Nov
   2026), the 14,400-point map returns 89.4748 S, 137.545 W, 28 m from the maximum of the LOLA 60 m
   illumination map. Only 5 % of the ground within 30 km of the pole can host that mission on the planned
   date, and the largest slip margin anywhere is 9 days.
3. **At the pole the slip margin is set by the Earth, not the Sun.** On the connecting ridge the Sun is up
   for the whole 14-day window on every date tested; the verdict flips with the Earth's monthly swing
   (about 53 % of the year in view, outages of 13 days). A smooth sphere gets the number of good dates
   about right there (20 against 20 of 60) because of that. Away from the ridges it is badly wrong: at the
   IM-1 site a flat horizon finds 13 good dates of 60 from 18 Nov 2026 and terrain leaves 4; at IM-1's real
   landing hour, 9 against 1.
4. **Away from the pole a day of slip costs exactly 24 sunlit hours.** At the IM-2, IM-1, Chandrayaan-3
   and Blue Ghost sites the sunlit hours in the window fall by 24 for each further day of slip once the
   window reaches sunset, because the lunar day does not move with the landing. The slip margin is then simple arithmetic on the hours left, and
   the tool's value there is the date of the next window and the terrain correction to sunset.
5. **The ridge-pixel figure reproduces.** The review's 4.3 points on well-lit pixels came out at 4.0
   (mean difference -2.8, correlation 0.61) with a different seed. The blocker of the 198-hour dark run is
   0.24 to 0.40 km away, as the review said. The 0 m crest values fall from 75.5 to 42.7 % as the first
   ray sample moves from 80 m to 10 m, to the decimal the review gave.
6. **The Sun check and the Earth check really use the same points.** The Earth run's "any Sun" column
   averages 20.39 and 29.68 %, the computed means of the stored Sun validation. The Earth side is ready:
   within 5 degrees of the pole the Earth's centre is in view 22.2 % of the time at the 200 random points.

## Easier than expected

- The mission card and the scatter band were a thin layer over the existing slip grid: 13 skylines per
  site are cached on disk, and a warm card takes 0.04 s.
- The slip map did not need a pre-computed picture per date. Storing the 14,400 skylines (21 MB, 23
  minutes once) and evaluating them live takes 4.2 s per new date, so the map follows the card's dates and
  need. A test shows the map value equals the mission card at the same point.
- Memory: the review's 700 MB came from temporary arrays in long spans and from 40 threads working at
  once. Blocks of two years, two computations at a time and returning freed memory brought the peak to
  296 MB on the 240 m configuration.

## Harder than expected, and dead ends

- A principled near-field rule does not exist at this resolution. Any first-sample distance below the
  grid spacing measures the bilinear interpolation. The convention is now named, reported with every
  skyline and included as one member of the "how sure" check, but 0 m crest values stay unconverged, and
  only finer terrain fixes that.
- Speeding up the map with threads gained nothing (NumPy's gathers here are memory-bound); the saving came
  from an exact shortcut that skips the solar-disc strips where the whole disc is clear of every skyline
  sample it can touch.
- Mission-specific requirements could not be verified. Two web fetches returned landing times but no
  planned surface duration for IM-1, IM-2 or Blue Ghost, so the four real-landing examples share one
  illustrative need and say so. A domain expert on the team could replace it with real requirements.
- The Earth maps' file names are second-hand. The download script tries three addresses per file and the
  reader matches any name containing AVGVISIB, the region and EARTH, but the first real run may need a
  manual download.

## What competitors already do (from the review; not re-read this round)

Of five public entries read, four ray-cast LOLA terrain, two on 20 m and 5 m grids, three have live
sites, one has a public video. One has a two-scenario "delay by 72 hours" button, one a 29-date search, one
a landing-dispersion check for single scenarios, one reports a map statistic against the LOLA illumination
product (1.45 points). None read gives a slip margin, a verdict per day of slip, a spread on a grid of
dates, a schedule-robustness map or an Earth-map check. More than 50 repositories exist for this
challenge and were pushed to within the last two days, so that list can be out of date by Sunday.

## Risks for submission

- No live link yet, and competitors have them. Deployment is untested; the 80 m grid does not fit GitHub
  (231 MB) and a free host will run the 240 m configuration, whose numbers are not the validated ones. The
  page says so, but a judge may not read that far. A host with 1 GB and a slightly larger disk removes it.
- A free host with a fraction of a CPU will take tens of seconds for the first slip map of a date
  (`SLIPDAY_FIELD_STRIDE=2` quarters the work). The first screen does not depend on the map.
- The Earth check is the largest open validity item and depends on a download that has not been tried.
- The data files were copied from other teams' and unrelated repositories. They are NASA's files by hash,
  but the submission should cite the official sources after re-fetching them.
- The verdict words are strong. "Yes" means the geometry model is stable against landing position and
  grid, not that a mission is safe. The page and LIMITATIONS.md say this; the video caption should not
  overstate it.
- Team rule: Universal teams need a second member, and the participant is still recruiting.

## Frank view of the ceiling

The entry now has one idea that is easy to say and that no public competitor read shows: a slip margin
with a stated confidence, on real terrain, for real missions. That is a credible route to a nomination
among Universal entries if it is deployed and the video is made. It will not win on spectacle: there is no
3-D view and the terrain is two generations coarser than the best entries. Against a team with 20 m
terrain, slopes, power and a polished globe, Slipday wins only if judges weigh validity and relevance to
"re-planning after schedule slips" over breadth. With the Earth check run and a live link, a fair
expectation is strong on validity and relevance, average on presentation and influence. A global award is
unlikely; Best Use of Data is the only category where the validation work would count directly.
